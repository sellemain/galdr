# Jinjer - Pisces

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQNtGoM3FVU

The song opens by offering the body a place to stand. The pulse is already there, firm but not blunt, a clean moving floor under a warm surface. Nothing feels casual, but nothing attacks yet. The clean voice enters close and controlled, bright enough to seem unguarded, steady enough to make that softness suspect. It is not fragile singing. It is a held posture.

The first words step into light before the band reveals the room’s real size. *"Step forward and meet a new sunrise"* does not arrive as uplift so much as instruction. The phrase has space around it; the pressure below stays measured, the surface open, the line almost graceful. Attention settles because the music lets it settle. The clean voice keeps rounding the edges of the song, drawing suffering into shape rather than spilling it. When she sings of being *"a friend of mine / Who swallows suffering with smile,"* the arrangement has already made the smile audible as discipline. It is not relief. It is control.

That control becomes the trap. Through the first minute, the song keeps its pocket clean and deceptively comfortable, a progressive glide with enough weight underneath to warn against trusting the surface. The band pressure is present but leashed. The clean voice moves across it with a liquid precision, and the contrast keeps tightening because the music refuses to announce its violence early. It lets the listener learn the pattern first. It gives the body a rhythm it can believe in.

At 1:13, the floor opens.

The harsh vocal body does not feel like a new character walking in. It feels like the same organism exposing the part that was folded under the clean line. The band does not merely get louder around it; the whole surface changes its purpose. The pulse that had been carrying the clean melody now becomes pressure-bearing infrastructure, and the voice that enters is physical, close-grained, forced through the room with mass. It does not decorate the song’s heaviness. It is heaviness given a throat.

The lyric turns into divided biology: *"One being brings life / Another runs for death."* By then the sound has already split the body in two. Clean voice and harsh body are not beauty versus ugliness, not innocence versus rage in a simple costume change. They are different pressure systems inside one track. The clean line can still hover; the harsh vocal can still articulate; the band holds both without letting either resolve the other. This is where the song stops feeling like a reveal and starts feeling like an anatomy.

After the first rupture, the return to cleaner motion is not a retreat. It is more dangerous because the listener now knows what the smoothness contains. The groove stays settled, almost elegant, and the clean voice resumes with a poise that feels chosen under threat. *"No promises I ever give / Don’t rely on me and I won’t deceive"* lands with that cool refusal: the music does not plead innocence. It keeps moving, fin and tail, through a current that the band keeps tightening from below.

The middle stretch has a strange calm for a metal song this severe. The pressure is sustained more than constantly escalating. That matters. The band does not chase impact every bar; it holds the listener in a long corridor where clean tone, harsh memory, and weighted pulse keep exchanging authority. The body is captured but not slammed flat. There is enough comfort in the groove to make the next pressure change feel structural rather than merely loud.

Around 3:16, weight gathers under the moving pulse. The song darkens without losing its forward shape. The clean voice reappears in a more haunted register of meaning: *"Neptune’s child shivering inside / Drowns in the liquid gold."* The water imagery belongs because the music has become fluid in a hostile way. It does not wash; it contains. The band pressure thickens around the vocal line, and the clean voice sounds less like escape than suspension inside the element that will not release it.

From there the song keeps changing density while refusing collapse. The harsh body returns as a force of compression, and the band answers by making the current harder to swim through. Near 3:52 the weight lifts for a moment, not as rescue, but as a change in angle. The surface opens just enough for the next motion to cut more cleanly. The vocal roles are now fully separate in function: the clean voice carries contour, image, and eerie balance; the harsh voice gives the hidden violence a body; the band is the pressure field that decides how much space either one gets.

When the final current arrives, the song names the motion it has been enacting all along: *"Pisces swimming through the river / All their life against the stream."* The line works because the arrangement has never stopped pushing against its own glide. The pulse moves forward, but every return of weight makes forward motion feel opposed. The listener is not watching a fish symbol from outside. The music has made swimming into resistance, and resistance into meter.

The ending turns crueler, more exposed. The clean surface is no longer enough to soften the image of hooks, tables, knives, and ripped-away emeralds. The band gathers one last body-pressure under the line, then lets the structure begin to break at the edge. The harshness has done its work by then; even where the voice is not tearing the air, the song remembers it. The final pressure releases, the pattern fractures, and the body’s hold loosens into the closing cutoff.

Pisces leaves behind the feeling of a beautiful mechanism revealing its teeth from the inside. Its central shock is not that a clean singer can become harsh. It is that the clean voice, the harsh vocal body, and the band’s pressure all belong to the same current, and the song spends five minutes making the listener inhabit that current until the stream finally takes the shape away.
