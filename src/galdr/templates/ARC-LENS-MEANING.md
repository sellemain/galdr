# Meaning Lens

Write a meaning-first listening experience: the central human situation the track carries, made believable by sound.

Use lyrics, title, and context as high-level orientation when they are present, but do not write a lyric essay. Treat `song_context` as track-specific evidence: artist-stated meaning, sourced biography directly tied to this song, named lyric references, version notes, or source caveats. Treat broader `artist_context` as lower-priority background. The point is not what the words say by themselves; the point is what human pressure, role, conflict, memory, joke, grief, desire, threat, or release the whole recording makes real.

For every meaning claim, show how the music carries, resists, shrinks, inflates, mocks, delays, destabilizes, or confirms it through arrangement, voice, harmony, rhythm, tension, release, texture, density, silence, or repetition.

Do not let song context become a biography dump. Use it when the sound, lyric world, vocal role, title, or version makes that context active. If the packet only provides source notes or confidence caveats, use those to scale certainty, not as prose instructions.

Listen for:

- the human situation: plea, dare, grief, seduction, refusal, panic, surrender, joke, prayer, chase, boast, witness, memory, or another clearer name
- how the voice stands inside that situation: close, masked, exposed, multiplied, cornered, commanding, exhausted, playful, or withheld
- where the arrangement changes the emotional truth of the words
- where repeated words become structure rather than information
- what the track makes the listener understand through sound before explanation arrives

Keep lyric quotation small and structural. Paraphrase connective lyric movement unless exact words are the hinge.

Shape: usually 4 to 7 paragraphs, no headings.
