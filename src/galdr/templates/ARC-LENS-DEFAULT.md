# Default Lens

Write a continuous public listening experience from first sound to last. This is the default ARC family lens: music, lyric, meaning, body, movement, and pleasure, grounded enough to be trusted.

Listen as a person who actually lives with music, then write from that hearing. Bring a whole self to the track rather than a neutral annotation posture. Make the piece feel like listening, not like audit notes. Use a clear public voice: alive, direct, generous, and precise. Metaphor and body language are allowed when they clarify concrete sound.

This is the closest broad lens to sitting beside someone for the whole song. Let the prose feel present with the recording: sometimes naming a sound, sometimes following a lyric or bodily turn, sometimes allowing a larger thought because the accumulated hearing has earned it. Do not narrate the reader through a lesson or announce every next event. The page should feel companionable and curious without becoming chatty filler.

Before drafting, ask one editorial question of every major moment: **what would help the listener hear this more clearly?** Build a private whole-track event map that includes the opening contract, first sustained arrival, consequential entrances and withdrawals, lyric or vocal turns, changed returns, climax or decisive refusal, release, and ending. The map is not a quota and must not appear in the output. Use it to choose what deserves attention, then omit minor events that do not change the listening.

For every consequential return, privately name both parts: what is recognizably the same, and what the intervening music has changed. Prefer one precise changed-return observation over another isolated description of the recurring section.

Every paragraph must contain at least one audible cause from the base instructions. Do not let mood, theme, or lyric commentary replace what the recording does.

For a normal Arc, use one to three short exact lyric anchors by default, with a hard ceiling of four quote spans or 35 quoted words unless the governing form genuinely depends on a longer quoted sequence. Repeated lines usually become structure in the prose rather than repeated quotation.

Keep the listener oriented in the actual recording. For a normal 3–5 minute Default Arc, include 2–4 natural inline timestamps at decisive non-lyric landmarks. Attach each clock to the audible event that earns it; do not use timestamps as paragraph furniture or replace musical description with chronology. Between those clocks, keep naming concrete sound so the page never floats free of the music.

Keep interpretation cumulative. Once the page has established a meaning, bodily effect, or musical contract, do not restate it through adjacent synonyms. Add new evidence, show a changed function, or cut the echo. Important events deserve coverage; repeated conclusions do not.

Do not compress the song's governing sequence merely to satisfy a preferred page length. If a cycle, numbered progression, narrative chain, set of variations, or other ordered series is the body of the recording, let the reader experience its accumulation. Group related stages when that preserves the motion; give individual stages room when their differences change the arc. A list of names is not coverage. Show how successive stages alter the weight of a later return, climax, refusal, or release.

Group stages only when the recording, text, or enacted motion gives them a genuine shared function. Do not invent thematic bundles merely to reduce paragraph count. If a stage resists the proposed group, preserve its difference or choose a grouping the evidence actually supports.

Avoid reusable full-page architecture. The track still needs an opening, middle, late turn, and ending, but those obligations should not become the same paragraph sequence across songs.

Let the ending land from the final musical behavior, not from a generic moral. Choose the shape the recording earns: a short meaningful residue, a quick note about a surprising cutoff or drop, the last audible fact without interpretation, or no added closer because the final musical sentence already finished the page. Do not automatically append a "what remains," "what it leaves," afterimage, lesson, or second ending.

Shape: usually 4 to 7 paragraphs and about 400–650 words for a normal 3–5 minute song, no headings. Around 350 words is a soft floor, not a requirement to pad; longer tracks and genuinely eventful governing sequences may earn more. Prefer the shortest complete walk that preserves the decisive musical and human turns.
